§ 8

The American Revolution

Causes, course, and consequences of the war between Great Britain and her thirteen American colonies, 1775–1783.

By the spring of seventeen seventy-six, the political break between the thirteen American colonies and Great Britain had become irreconcilable. The dispute, which had begun over the right to tax, had expanded within a decade to questions of sovereignty, representation, and the meaning of subjecthood itself. The First Continental Congress, meeting in Philadelphia in September 1774, had attempted a petition to the Crown. The petition went unanswered.

The Second Continental Congress, which would become both a governing body and a wartime assembly, was already meeting when, on the night of April 18, militia riders set out from Boston to warn the countryside that British regulars were marching to seize a cache of arms at Concord.

The war that followed would last eight years, span a continent, and end in a victory so improbable that the most serious contemporary observers had given the colonial cause no chance of surviving the first campaign.

The Declaration, issued the following July, was less a legal document than a statement of facts submitted to a candid world. Its longest section enumerated not the wrongs of Parliament but of the King himself — a rhetorical move that would shape the new nation's self-understanding for the next two and a half centuries.
